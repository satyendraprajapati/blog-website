---

Most data slides get titled after the chart, not the conclusion — "Q3 Revenue by Region," "Customer Churn Trend," "Support Ticket Volume." Technically accurate, but they tell the audience what they're about to look at, not what they should take away from it. BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front — means the title carries the finding, and the chart underneath backs it up.

**Ask "so what?" before you finalize any title.** "Q3 Revenue by Region" answers "what is this chart" but not "why does it matter." Push one step further: what does the chart actually show, and what should the reader do with that? "West Region Revenue Fell 12% in Q3" answers both — it's the headline, and the chart is the evidence.

**Write the title as a complete sentence, not a label.** A label like "Churn Trend" could mean churn is up, down, or flat — the reader has to look at the chart to find out. A sentence like "Churn Dropped for the Third Straight Month" removes the ambiguity before they've even looked.

**State the number if it's the point.** "Support Tickets Are Up" is directionally true but forgettable. "Support Tickets Rose 34% After the App Update" is specific enough that someone skimming just the title slide-by-slide still gets the real story — which matters, because a lot of readers do exactly that with a deck they weren't in the room for.

**Keep the topic version in the chart's axis or caption, not the title.** You still want "Revenue by Region, Q3 2026" somewhere for context — just demote it to a subtitle or axis label under the headline, instead of letting it take the title slot the insight should occupy.

**Be honest when the finding is "nothing changed."** BLUF doesn't mean manufacturing drama out of flat data. "Retention Held Steady at 87% Despite the Price Change" is a legitimate, useful headline — it tells a stakeholder they don't need to worry about that number this quarter, which is exactly the kind of takeaway a title should deliver.

**Read the deck's titles alone, without the charts.** A fast gut-check: strip every slide down to just its title and read them in sequence. If that sequence tells a coherent story on its own — problem, evidence, recommendation — the deck is doing its job. If it reads like a table of contents, the titles are still describing topics instead of findings.

**Apply it last, after the analysis is settled.** Don't force a headline before you're confident in the number — a title that overstates what the data actually shows is worse than a boring one. Write the descriptive version first while you're still exploring, then rewrite to BLUF once you know what the slide is actually claiming.

The chart doesn't change under any of this — same bars, same lines, same table. What changes is whether someone who only reads titles walks away knowing what happened, or just knowing what topic was covered.
