---

Writing speaker notes for a data readout is usually the last thing that gets done before a meeting, and it's often the part that gets skipped entirely — which is how analysts end up reading numbers straight off a slide instead of explaining them. PowerPoint's Dictate feature turns that writing task into a talking task, which is faster for most people and produces notes that actually sound like something you'd say out loud.

**1. Find it on the Home tab.** Dictate lives in the **Home** ribbon (and also on the **Record** tab in some builds), usually near the right-hand side with a microphone icon. Click it once to start listening — no setup, add-in, or account connection required beyond a working microphone.

**2. Click into the Notes pane before you start talking.** Dictate transcribes into whatever text field currently has focus. If you click the microphone while a slide body placeholder is selected, your narration lands on the slide itself, not in the notes — for a readout, click into the **Notes** pane at the bottom of Normal view first, then start Dictate.

**3. Talk through the slide the way you'd actually present it.** Rather than trying to compose tidy sentences, describe the chart out loud as if a colleague just asked "what's this slide showing?" — "so this is the regional breakdown, West is up 18% year over year, that's mostly driven by the new accounts we signed in March." That rougher, spoken phrasing is exactly what makes decent speaker notes — notes written in formal prose tend to read stilted when you glance down at them mid-sentence during the actual meeting.

**4. Use voice commands for basic punctuation instead of stopping to type it.** Saying "comma," "period," "new line," or "new paragraph" inserts the corresponding punctuation without breaking your flow — useful for numbers-heavy narration where you're listing out several figures in a row and want them on separate lines for easy scanning while presenting.

**5. Go slide by slide and stop the mic between them.** Click Dictate again (or say nothing and let it auto-pause) to stop before moving to the next slide, then click into that slide's Notes pane and start again. Trying to narrate an entire ten-slide deck in one continuous pass makes it much harder to catch and fix a misheard number before moving on.

**6. Proofread numbers specifically before you present.** Dictation tools are generally solid with regular speech but inconsistent with spoken numbers, currency, and percentages — "eighteen percent" sometimes becomes "18%" and sometimes becomes "18 percent" or even "A teen percent" depending on how clearly it was said. Since these notes exist specifically to support a data readout, a quick pass checking every number against the actual chart is worth doing before the notes are trusted in front of an audience.

**7. Treat the output as a first draft, not a final script.** The real win isn't perfectly polished notes — it's skipping the blank-page problem of staring at an empty Notes pane for ten slides. Talking through each slide once, out loud, gets you 80% of a usable script in a fraction of the time typing would take, and editing rough text down is almost always faster than writing it from nothing.
