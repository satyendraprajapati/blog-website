title: "Building a KPI Dashboard Slide in PowerPoint Without Add-Ins"

excerpt: "A row of stat cards showing revenue, margin, and growth at a glance is one of the highest-value slides in a data deck — here's how to lay one out cleanly using only native shapes and alignment tools."
---

Before a deck gets into charts and detail, most stakeholders want the headline numbers first: revenue, margin, growth, whatever the three or four metrics of the review actually are. A row of KPI cards does that job better than a bullet list or a table, and you don't need a dashboard add-in to build one — native shapes, alignment guides, and a bit of restraint get you there.

**1. Start from a grid, not a blank slide.** Turn on *View → Guides* and place vertical guides to divide the slide into equal columns — three or four, matching however many KPIs you're showing. Building each card inside its own column from the start keeps spacing consistent, instead of eyeballing gaps between shapes and fixing misalignment later.

**2. Build one card as a single rectangle, then duplicate it.** Draw one rounded rectangle, set its fill to a light neutral or your brand's card background color, and add two text boxes inside it: a large number and a smaller label beneath it. Get the spacing, font sizes, and colors right on this one card first, group the three objects together, then copy it across the row — this guarantees every card is visually identical instead of subtly different sizes.

**3. Make the number the biggest thing on the card, by far.** The metric value should be at least twice the point size of its label — something like 40pt for the number against 14pt for the label below it. A KPI card's entire job is letting someone read the number from across the room; if the label competes visually with the number, the card has failed at its one purpose.

**4. Use color on the number to carry meaning, not decoration.** A green number for "on target" and a red one for "below target" lets a viewer scan four cards and know which ones need attention before you say a word. Keep this restrained to at most two or three colors total across the row — a card row where every number is a different color loses the signal, because nothing stands out as the exception anymore.

**5. Add a small trend indicator instead of a second chart.** A tiny up or down triangle glyph next to a percentage change (▲ 4.2% or ▼ 1.8%) gives direction at a glance without needing a full sparkline or mini-chart crammed into the card. Insert it as a text character rather than a separate shape, so it stays perfectly aligned with the text baseline next to it.

**6. Align every card to the same baseline using Align, not by dragging.** Select all the card groups and use *Format → Align → Align Middle* (for a horizontal row) to guarantee pixel-perfect alignment. A row of KPI cards that are off by a few pixels vertically reads as sloppy even if nobody in the room can articulate why — Align fixes it in one click instead of nudging shapes with arrow keys.

**7. Leave the cards static and update them by hand, unless the deck refreshes weekly.** For a one-off readout, typing updated numbers into the text boxes each time is simpler and less fragile than linking every card to an external data source. If this deck genuinely refreshes on a recurring schedule, that's the point to consider linking the underlying chart data from Excel instead of rebuilding the cards manually each time — but don't add that complexity for a deck you're only presenting once.

The result is a slide that answers "how are we doing" before anyone has to read a chart — and because every card was built from one duplicated template, it holds together visually even if you didn't think of yourself as a slide designer going in.
