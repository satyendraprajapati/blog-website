Not every visual on a data slide needs to be a chart, and not every icon needs to be built by hand out of Merge Shapes. PowerPoint ships with a searchable library of vector icons and royalty-free stock photography under **Insert**, and most analysts never open it.

**1. Search Insert > Icons by concept, not by exact word.** Go to **Insert > Icons** and search for the idea you're illustrating — "growth," "warning," "target," "calendar" — rather than a literal term. The library returns simple, scalable vector icons designed to sit cleanly next to text or numbers, and because they're vector, they resize on a slide without ever going blurry the way a dragged-in PNG icon would.

**2. Recolor icons to match your deck's theme instead of leaving them default black.** An inserted icon becomes an editable **Graphic** object — select it and the **Graphics Format** tab appears, with a **Graphics Fill** option that recolors the whole icon to any color, including your theme's accent colors. A black-and-white icon dropped onto a branded deck reads as an afterthought; a re-colored one reads as part of the design.

**3. Use icons as compact labels on KPI or dashboard-style slides.** On a slide with several metric tiles (revenue, churn, active users), a small icon above or beside each number gives a viewer a half-second visual anchor for what they're looking at before they even read the label — faster to scan than text alone, and lighter-weight than building a SmartArt diagram just to label four boxes.

**4. Ungroup an icon when you need to recolor just one part of it.** Icons import as a single grouped graphic, but right-clicking one and choosing **Convert to Shape** breaks it into its individual paths. That's useful when, say, a trend-arrow icon needs its arrowhead in your alert color while the rest stays neutral — something the single whole-icon fill in tip #2 can't do on its own.

**5. Reach for Insert > Pictures > Stock Images only for photography, and mainly on non-data slides.** The same library includes royalty-free stock photos, separate from the icon set. These work well on a title slide or a section divider where you want a human, on-brand image — they work poorly next to an actual chart or table, where a decorative photo competes with the data for attention instead of supporting it.

**6. Know when to skip icons entirely.** A slide whose whole job is presenting a chart doesn't need a decorative icon fighting for the viewer's eye. Save icons for the slides where they're doing real information-architecture work — labeling a KPI tile, marking a section header, or acting as a small wayfinding cue on a menu slide — not sprinkling visual interest onto a slide that should be carrying its weight with the data itself.

Nothing here requires a design background or an outside asset library — it's a tab most analysts scroll past on the way to Insert > Chart. For the slides that do call for a bit of visual labeling, it's worth the extra ten seconds to check it before reaching for a generic clip-art search or skipping the visual cue altogether.
