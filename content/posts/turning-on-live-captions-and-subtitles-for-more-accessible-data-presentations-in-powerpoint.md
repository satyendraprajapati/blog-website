Alt text on charts helps someone reading the deck afterward, but it does nothing for a live audience member who's deaf, hard of hearing, or just joining from a noisy room. PowerPoint's Live Captions feature transcribes what you say in real time and displays it on the slide as you present — a feature most analysts never turn on because it's buried a few clicks deep.

**1. Turn it on from the Slide Show tab, not Settings.** Go to Slide Show → Always Use Subtitles, then use the dropdown next to it to set your spoken language and the caption language (they can differ, since PowerPoint can translate on the fly). Once checked, captions appear automatically every time you start the slideshow — no separate step needed at presentation time.

**2. Pick caption position based on where your data actually lives on the slide.** The same Slide Show tab lets you choose where captions appear — below the slide, overlaid at the bottom, or overlaid at the top. For a data-heavy slide with a chart and takeaway text near the bottom, overlaying captions at the top avoids covering the numbers you're actively pointing to.

**3. Test it with your own voice and terminology before presenting live.** Live Captions relies on speech recognition, and it stumbles on the same things any speech-to-text does: acronyms, product names, and numbers spoken quickly. Run through your actual talk track once with captions on — "year-over-year" and specific metric names are common misfires — so you know where it struggles instead of finding out mid-presentation.

**4. Keep sentences shorter when captions are on.** This is a delivery habit, not a setting: speech recognition catches up more accurately at natural pauses, so a presenter who talks in shorter sentences with brief pauses between them gets noticeably cleaner captions than one running sentences together for thirty seconds at a stretch.

**5. Use the translation option deliberately, not by default.** If you set a spoken language different from the caption language, PowerPoint translates in real time. This is genuinely useful for a multilingual stakeholder audience, but translation adds a small delay and another point where meaning can drift — for a same-language audience, leave both settings identical and skip the translation layer entirely.

**6. Remember this is a live-only feature — it doesn't touch the file afterward.** Live Captions only appear during an active slideshow; they aren't saved into the `.pptx`, embedded as a transcript, or available to anyone opening the file later. For a recorded or emailed version of the same deck, you still need separate captions or alt text — Live Captions solves the in-the-room accessibility gap, not the after-the-fact one.

None of this requires an add-in or internet-dependent service beyond what's already built into PowerPoint — just a checkbox that's easy to miss, and worth turning on by default before any presentation with an audience you haven't personally vetted for hearing needs.
