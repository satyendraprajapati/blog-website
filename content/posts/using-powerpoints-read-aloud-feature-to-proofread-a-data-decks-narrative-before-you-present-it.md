title: "Using PowerPoint's Read Aloud Feature to Proofread a Data Deck's Narrative Before You Present It"
date: "2026-10-26"
tags: ["powerpoint", "presenting", "beginner"]
excerpt: "Why listening to your own slide titles and takeaways read back to you catches awkward phrasing and typos that silent proofreading misses."
---

Most proofreading of a data deck happens the same way the deck was written — silently, scanning the slide with your eyes already knowing what it's supposed to say. That's exactly why a clunky sentence or a typo in a headline number slips through: you're reading what you meant, not what's actually on the slide. PowerPoint's built-in Read Aloud feature reads the text back to you instead, and hearing it exposes problems silent reading doesn't.

**1. Find it under Review, not Slide Show.** `Review → Read Aloud` reads the text of the currently selected slide (or a selected text box) out loud using your system's text-to-speech voice. It's easy to miss because it lives next to spelling and accessibility tools, not anywhere near the presenting features.

**2. Run it on your slide titles and takeaway lines first.** These are the sentences an audience reads fastest and judges hardest — a title like "Revenue Declined 12% Driven By West Region Churn" needs to land cleanly on first read. Hearing it spoken reveals a different problem than spotting a typo: you notice when a sentence is too long to say in one breath, or when the key number is buried in the middle instead of leading.

**3. Listen for how a number actually sounds, not just how it's formatted.** A chart label reading "$1.284B" reads fine silently, but hearing it spoken alongside a sentence like "...which drove 1.284B in losses" exposes that nobody says a number that way out loud — you'd say "about $1.3 billion" when presenting it live. If the slide text and your actual spoken delivery don't match, that's worth fixing on the slide, since someone reading the deck later without you narrating it will take the written number literally.

**4. Catch acronym and jargon overload before an outside audience does.** Read Aloud pronounces everything literally, including acronyms your team uses daily but a stakeholder from another department doesn't — hearing "CAC payback improved QoQ" read flatly, with no vocal emphasis to carry meaning the way you would live, makes it obvious which terms need spelling out or a footnote for an audience that isn't as deep in the data as you are.

**5. Use it on speaker notes too, not just visible slide text.** Select the Notes pane and run Read Aloud there — this is the closer rehearsal for what you'll actually say live, and it surfaces the same awkward phrasing problems in your talking points that it does in slide text, before you're saying them out loud for the first time in front of an actual audience.

**6. Adjust the voice and speed from the Read Aloud settings, not your system's.** A small gear or settings icon appears in the Read Aloud toolbar once it's running, letting you change the reading speed and voice without digging into your OS's accessibility settings — slow it down the first pass through a dense, number-heavy slide, then speed it back up for a final listen once the wording is fixed.

**7. Don't treat it as a spell-checker replacement.** Read Aloud is for catching how something sounds and flows, not for catching every misspelling — a text-to-speech engine will happily pronounce "there" when you meant "their" since both are valid words. Run your normal spelling check first, then use Read Aloud as a second pass focused on rhythm, clarity, and whether the sentence actually says what you want an audience to walk away remembering.

It costs nothing and takes a couple of minutes per deck, but it catches a category of problem — not wrong, just clumsy — that's genuinely hard to hear in your own head no matter how many times you silently reread the same slide.
