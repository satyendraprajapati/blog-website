---

Alongside Googlebot and Bingbot, your blog's logs now almost certainly show hits from crawlers with names like `GPTBot`, `ClaudeBot`, `CCBot`, and `Google-Extended`. These aren't indexing your site for search results — they're collecting text to train or ground AI models. `robots.txt` is the one lever you have to say yes or no to each of them individually, and the default (no mention at all) quietly means yes to everyone.

**1. Know that this is a separate decision from your `llms.txt` file.** If you've already added an `llms.txt` to help AI *answer engines* find and cite your posts when someone asks a question, that's about discovery and citation at query time. AI *training* crawlers are a different concern entirely — whether a model gets to ingest your writing into its training data months or years before anyone asks it anything. You can opt into one and out of the other; they aren't the same toggle.

**2. Target each bot by its own user-agent, not a blanket rule.** `robots.txt` lets you write a separate block per crawler, which matters because these bots serve different purposes — blocking `GPTBot` doesn't block `ClaudeBot`, and blocking training crawlers doesn't touch Googlebot's indexing at all unless you write a rule for it too.
```
# OpenAI's training crawler
User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

# Anthropic's training crawler
User-agent: ClaudeBot
Disallow: /

# Common Crawl, a dataset many AI labs train on
User-agent: CCBot
Disallow: /

# Google's AI-training-specific crawler (separate from Googlebot's search indexing)
User-agent: Google-Extended
Disallow: /

# Everything else, including search engines
User-agent: *
Allow: /
```

**3. Decide based on what you actually want, not a default stance.** There's a real tradeoff here, not an obviously correct answer. Blocking every AI crawler keeps your writing out of future training runs, but it also means your posts won't surface when someone asks an AI assistant a question your blog happens to answer well — the opposite of what `llms.txt` is trying to achieve for answer engines. A personal blog built partly to demonstrate expertise publicly might reasonably allow discovery-oriented crawlers while blocking pure-training ones; there's no setting that's right for everyone.

**4. Check this file in before your next deploy, not after noticing a crawler in your logs.** Since this project's `public/robots.txt` already gets generated by `npm run generate-feeds` before every build, add the AI crawler blocks to whatever source file or script produces it, so they survive the next regeneration instead of getting silently overwritten by a version that doesn't know about them.

**5. Remember `robots.txt` is a request, not a lock.** It's an honor-system convention — well-behaved crawlers from major labs do respect it, which is why this works at all, but a disallow rule can't technically stop a scraper that ignores it. If you need enforcement rather than a polite opt-out, that's a server- or firewall-level block on the user-agent string, not something `robots.txt` alone can guarantee.

**6. Re-check the list periodically.** New crawlers with new names show up as AI labs build new products — Meta's, Perplexity's, and others' bots already have their own user-agent strings, and more will follow. A `robots.txt` written once in 2026 is already a snapshot of that moment; revisit it every so often rather than assuming the list you wrote is still complete a year later.

Whichever way you decide, the point is that it's a decision now, not an accident — the blank default isn't neutral, it's an open door that happens to have no sign on it.
