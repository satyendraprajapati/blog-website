Red bars for "down," green bars for "up" is the most common color choice on a data slide, and it's also the one most likely to fail for a meaningful chunk of your audience. Red-green color blindness affects about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women — on a slide deck presented to a room of twenty people, the odds that someone can't distinguish your two key colors are higher than most presenters assume.

**1. Stop defaulting to red and green as your only signal.** If a chart's entire meaning depends on distinguishing red from green, redesign it so color is reinforcing the message, not carrying it alone. Pair color with a second cue — a "+12%" label, an up/down arrow icon, or position on the chart — so the data still reads correctly even if the color difference doesn't land.

**2. Swap red/green for blue/orange.** Blue and orange sit far enough apart on the color spectrum that they stay distinguishable across all common types of color vision deficiency, and they carry the same "these are two different, comparable things" meaning without any of red/green's associations breaking down. It's a one-line change in most cases: recolor your "increase" series orange and your "decrease" series blue instead of the default green and red.

**3. Never rely on color alone in a legend-only chart.** If a chart has more than two or three categories and the only way to tell them apart is a small color swatch in a legend, add data labels directly on or next to each segment instead. Direct labeling also removes the back-and-forth eye movement between chart and legend that slows every viewer down, colorblind or not.

**4. Use PowerPoint's built-in check before you present.** Insert → Add-ins → and searching for a color contrast or accessibility checker will flag low-contrast text and color pairs directly in your slide, or you can use the Accessibility Checker under the Review tab, which flags some contrast issues alongside its usual missing-alt-text warnings. It won't catch every colorblindness scenario, but it catches the most common failure — text or a thin data line that's too close in luminance to its background to read at a glance.

**5. Test your actual palette, not just your intent.** A color combination that looks fine described on paper ("we used blue and orange") can still fail if the specific shades you picked are too close in brightness. Screenshot your chart and run it through a free colorblindness simulator (searching "colorblind simulator" turns up several browser-based tools) before the deck goes out — it takes two minutes and catches problems no amount of "I think this is fine" review will.

**6. Keep a standard palette across the whole deck.** Once you've settled on a colorblind-safe set — say, a blue/orange pair for comparisons and a single sequential blue scale for magnitude — reuse it on every slide instead of picking fresh colors chart by chart. Consistency does double duty here: it's accessible by design, and it also means your audience learns "blue means X" once and doesn't have to relearn the color key on every new slide.

None of this requires redesigning your whole deck — most fixes are a recolor and a label, not a rebuild. The return is a chart that communicates correctly to everyone in the room, not just the majority who happen to see red and green as different colors.
