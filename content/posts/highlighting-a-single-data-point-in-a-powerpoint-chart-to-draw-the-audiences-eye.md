title: "Highlighting a Single Data Point in a PowerPoint Chart to Draw the Audience's Eye"
date: "2026-08-16"
tags: ["powerpoint", "data-storytelling", "beginner"]
excerpt: "A chart with twelve identical-colored bars makes the audience hunt for the point that matters — here's how to make the one bar the slide is actually about impossible to miss."
---

A slide title can say "Q3 dipped in the West region," but if the chart underneath it shows twelve bars in the same shade of blue, the audience still has to scan and find West themselves before the point lands. Highlighting the one data point the slide is actually about closes that gap — the eye goes straight to it instead of doing the work your title already did in words.

**1. Select the single data point, not the whole series.** Click once on the chart's data series to select all of it, then click a second time — slowly, not a double-click — on just the one bar, point, or slice you want to change. PowerPoint switches from "series selected" to "single point selected," which you can confirm in the Format pane header.

**2. Recolor that one point, and gray out the rest.** With the single point selected, change its fill to your accent color. Then select the rest of the series (Format Data Series, not Format Data Point) and set the fill to a neutral gray. The contrast between one saturated color and a field of gray does more work than any amount of bolding or circling.

**3. Use a data label on just that point, not the whole axis.** Right-click the highlighted point specifically and choose `Add Data Label` — this adds a label to that one point only, not the whole series. Combined with the recolor, the number itself becomes part of the highlight instead of competing with axis tick marks for attention.

**4. Reach for a text callout only when the bar's position alone isn't enough context.** If "this bar is lower" doesn't explain *why* it matters, a short text box with a thin connector line pointing at the bar — something like "West: down 12% after the March pricing change" — adds the missing context. Keep it to one line; a callout that requires its own reading time defeats the point of highlighting.

**5. Don't highlight more than one thing per chart.** The technique works because it removes ambiguity about where to look. Two highlighted bars on the same chart puts the audience back to comparing and guessing which one the slide is actually about — if you need to make two points, that's usually two slides, or two smaller charts side by side.

**6. Reset native theme colors before you present to a new audience.** A recolored point is saved with the chart, not the slide master, so if you duplicate this slide for a different month's readout, remember to reset the highlight to the new relevant bar rather than leaving last month's pointed at the wrong data.

This costs a few clicks per chart, but it's the difference between a slide the audience has to interpret and one that tells them exactly where to look before you've said a word.
