excerpt: "PowerPoint's Rehearse Timings feature records exactly how long you spend on each slide, so you can find and fix the slide that's eating your 15-minute meeting slot before you're in front of the room."
---

A data readout booked for 15 minutes that actually runs 25 isn't a delivery problem you can fix with better eye contact — it's a pacing problem, and the only way to see it clearly is to measure it. `Rehearse Timings` (on the Slide Show tab) does exactly that: it times a full run-through slide by slide and saves the numbers, so you find out where the deck runs long before a stakeholder does.

**1. Start it from Slide Show → Rehearse Timings, not a normal slide show.** This opens the same full-screen presenting view you'd use live, but with a running clock in the corner tracking total time and time on the current slide. Present it exactly as you would for real — don't rush through it faster than you'd actually talk, or the numbers won't mean anything.

**2. Let it run end to end, including the slides you think are quick.** It's tempting to skip ahead past the slides you're confident about, but those are often the ones secretly costing you time — a simple KPI slide you always add "one more thing" to live. Running the full rehearsal, every time, is what catches that.

**3. Review the per-slide breakdown before you touch anything in the deck.** When you end the rehearsal, PowerPoint offers to save the timings and shows total elapsed time. Switch to Slide Sorter view afterward — each slide's thumbnail displays its recorded duration underneath it, which turns "the deck ran long" into "slide 8 took four minutes by itself."

**4. Fix the slide, don't just cut the number.** Once you've found the slide eating your time budget, the fix is almost always to split dense content across two slides, move a tangent to an appendix slide, or pre-empt the question that always triggers a side discussion — not to mentally promise yourself you'll talk faster next time. Re-run the rehearsal after editing to confirm the change actually bought back the time you needed.

**5. Don't confuse this with the timings used for a self-running kiosk deck.** Rehearse Timings and automatic slide advance share the same underlying mechanism — PowerPoint can advance slides automatically using whatever durations you recorded — but the goal here is different: you're measuring your own live pacing against a meeting clock, not building an unattended loop for a lobby screen. Turn off "Use Timings" under Set Up Slide Show afterward if you don't want the deck to start auto-advancing the next time you present it live.

Run the rehearsal a day or two before the real readout, not five minutes beforehand — that gives you enough runway to actually restructure a slide that's running long, instead of just discovering the problem on your way into the room.
