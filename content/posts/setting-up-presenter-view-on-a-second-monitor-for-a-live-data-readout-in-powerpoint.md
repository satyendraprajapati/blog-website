---

Standing up to present a quarterly numbers deck and defaulting to "mirror displays" means the projector shows exactly what your laptop screen shows — including your notes, your task bar, and the next slide's ugly draft title. Presenter View exists precisely so the audience sees a clean, full-screen chart while you see everything you actually need to talk through it confidently.

**1. Connect the second display before opening PowerPoint.** Whether it's a projector, a conference room TV, or an actual second monitor, get Windows or macOS to recognize it first — PowerPoint reads the current display configuration when Slide Show starts, and connecting mid-presentation can force you to restart the show to pick up the new arrangement.

**2. Set the displays to Extend, not Duplicate.** On Windows, `Win + P` → "Extend"; on macOS, System Settings → Displays → uncheck "Mirror Displays". Extend gives PowerPoint two genuinely separate screens to put different content on — Duplicate forces both screens to show the same thing, which defeats Presenter View entirely.

**3. Tell PowerPoint which screen is the audience's.** Go to Slide Show → Set Up Slide Show, or check Slide Show → Monitor, and confirm the "Presenter View" checkbox is on and the correct monitor is assigned to the audience-facing output. PowerPoint usually guesses right, but it's worth confirming once rather than discovering the swap mid-presentation.

**4. Know the four things Presenter View gives you that the audience never sees.** Your current slide with a countdown timer, a preview of the next slide, your speaker notes in a readable font size (not the audience's tiny thumbnail), and a strip of all slides for jumping around without walking through them in order. For a data readout, the next-slide preview matters more than it sounds — knowing "the trend chart is coming right after this table" changes how you pace the sentence you're on.

**5. Use the zoom-in and pen tools that live only in Presenter View.** A magnifying glass icon lets you zoom into a specific region of a dense chart or table on the audience's screen — genuinely useful when a data table has a small number three rows down that the back of the room can't otherwise read — without needing to rebuild the slide with a bigger font.

**6. Have a one-screen fallback ready.** If you're presenting over a video call and screen-sharing your entire display, dual monitors work naturally — share the audience monitor and keep Presenter View on your own. But if you're sharing a single laptop screen with no second display available, use Slide Show → Presenter View's "Swap Presenter View and Slide Show" button, or fall back to plain Slide Show mode with printed or side-notes instead — some situations genuinely don't have a second screen, and it's worth deciding your fallback before you're standing in the room finding out.

**7. Rehearse the actual monitor setup, not just the slides.** The content of a data deck rarely goes wrong; the projector defaulting to Duplicate, or Presenter View landing on the wrong screen, is what actually eats the first two minutes of a live session. A five-minute test in the room — or with the exact adapter and cable you'll use — before the audience arrives catches that class of problem when it costs nothing instead of when it costs you your opening.

Presenter View doesn't change a single number in the deck — it changes whether you're reading your notes off a screen the audience can also see, or working from a private view that lets the audience's screen stay exactly as clean as the chart deserves.
