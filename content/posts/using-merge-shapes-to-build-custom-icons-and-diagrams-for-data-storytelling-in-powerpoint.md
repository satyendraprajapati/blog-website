excerpt: "How PowerPoint's Merge Shapes tool lets you build custom icons and process diagrams from basic shapes instead of settling for generic SmartArt or stock icon packs."
---

SmartArt covers generic process diagrams well, but the moment you need a specific icon — a custom funnel shape that matches your actual conversion stages, a badge highlighting one number on a slide, an arrow that points exactly where your chart's anomaly is — a stock icon library rarely has the exact shape, and a screenshot from elsewhere looks visibly out of place next to native PowerPoint objects. Merge Shapes builds that exact shape natively, out of the ordinary shapes already on your ribbon.

**1. Find it under Shape Format → Merge Shapes**, or add it to your Quick Access Toolbar if you'll use it often — it only becomes active once two or more shapes are selected. Select the shapes you want to combine, then pick one of five operations: Union, Combine, Fragment, Intersect, or Subtract.

**2. Use Subtract to cut a precise notch or window out of a shape.** Draw a rectangle, draw a small circle where you want a hole, select both (rectangle first, circle second), and choose Subtract. This is how you'd build a custom badge shape with a cut-out for a number, or a callout arrow with a clean chart-shaped window in it.

**3. Use Union to fuse separate shapes into one clean outline.** Overlap a rectangle and a triangle to build a simple custom arrow or pointer shape, select both, and Union merges them into a single shape with one fill and one outline — instead of two separate objects that drift apart the moment someone nudges one of them later.

**4. Use Intersect to keep only the overlapping area.** This is the fastest way to build a shape that has to fit exactly inside another — a rounded highlight that has to sit precisely inside a chart's plot area, for instance — without measuring coordinates by hand.

**5. Build your own KPI or process icons this way instead of hunting a stock pack for an exact match.** A funnel with your specific number of stages, a speedometer gauge, or a simple flow arrow with your own stage count are each two or three basic shapes and one merge operation — and because the result is a native PowerPoint shape, it inherits theme colors and resizes cleanly, unlike a pasted-in image.

**6. Group the result once you're happy with it**, so it behaves as one object in the Selection Pane instead of leaving loose shapes that can be separately dragged out of alignment by accident later.

**7. Keep the source shapes' fill and outline set before merging, not after.** The merged shape inherits its formatting from whichever shape was selected last, so set colors on the final shape you click before running the merge — it's faster than reformatting the combined result afterward.

The point isn't to turn every slide into custom vector art — it's that a handful of merge operations covers the specific-shape gap between "PowerPoint's defaults" and "a designer-made icon," using tools that are already on the ribbon and that stay editable as native shapes instead of a static image pasted in from somewhere else.
