title: "Building a Radar (Spider) Chart Natively in PowerPoint to Compare Multiple Metrics at Once"
date: "2026-10-01"
tags: ["powerpoint", "data-visualization", "beginner"]
excerpt: "When you need to compare several products or teams across half a dozen metrics at once, a radar chart fits more dimensions onto one slide than a bar chart ever could — and PowerPoint builds one natively."
---

A bar chart compares categories along one metric at a time. The moment a stakeholder wants to see how Region A and Region B stack up across five or six metrics simultaneously — satisfaction, revenue growth, retention, cost, delivery time — a bar chart turns into five or six separate charts, or one cluttered one. PowerPoint's radar chart puts every metric on its own axis radiating from a center point, so a full multi-metric profile shows up as a single shape per category.

**1. Insert it from Insert > Chart > Radar, not a workaround.** It's a genuine native chart type, listed in the chart-type gallery next to Waterfall and Funnel — there's no need to fake it with stacked shapes the way a Gantt or Sankey view on a data slide does.

**2. Structure your data table with metrics as rows, categories as columns.** Each axis on the radar corresponds to one row of the data table, and each shape traced on the chart corresponds to one column — so if you're comparing three products across five metrics, you want five rows and three columns, which is the opposite orientation from how most people default to laying out a table.

**3. Use "Filled Radar" sparingly, and only for one or two categories.** The filled variant shades the area inside each shape, which reads clearly for a single category or a simple two-way comparison, but with three or more overlapping filled shapes, the fills stack into a muddy mess and the audience can't tell which edge belongs to which color. Plain "Radar with Markers" — just the outline and points, no fill — holds up much better once you're past two categories.

**4. Normalize your metrics before you chart them.** A radar chart's axes all share the same scale from the center out, so plotting "revenue in millions" next to "satisfaction score out of 5" on the same chart collapses the satisfaction axis to a flat line near the center. Convert each metric to a 0–100 index, a percentile rank, or a percent-of-target first, so every axis is comparably scaled.

**5. Cap it at five or six axes, and two or three categories.** Past that, the shape stops reading as a recognizable profile and becomes a dense cobweb nobody can parse at presentation distance. If you need more dimensions than that, it's usually a sign the slide wants to be two radar charts (grouped by theme) rather than one with ten axes.

Used within those limits, a radar chart does something a bar or line chart structurally can't: it lets an audience see a whole profile's shape at a glance, and spot which category is strong everywhere versus which one has one standout metric propping up an otherwise average profile.
